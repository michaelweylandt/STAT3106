Course Description

Applied Machine Learning (STAT UN3106) is a lecture course meant to follow Introduction to Applied Data Science (STAT UN2108). Building on a foundation of classical linear and logistic regression models, core principles of modern statistical learning will be covered: cross-validation, resampling methods, regularization, model selection, ridge and lasso regression, margin-based classifiers, semi-parametric and kernel methods, tree-based methods, ensemble methods and neural networks. The primary focus of this course will be on the implementation and application of these methods, not formal theory.

Students will complete the course with a strong conceptual understanding the fundamentals of statistical machine learning and a practical understanding of many of the most useful methods of modern machine learning.

Textbooks & Course Materials

The primary textbook for this course will be An Introduction to Statistical Learning with Applications in Python (ISLP) by Gareth James, Daniela Witten, Trevor Hastie, Robert Tibshirani, and Jonathan Taylor. This textbook is freely available online at https://www.statlearning.com.

Additional readings will be assigned by the instructor from the following texts, all of which are freely available online:

  • Patterns, Predictions, and Actions: A story about machine learning by Moritz Hardt and Benjamin Recht
  • Fairness and Machine Learning: Limitations and Opportunities by Solon Barocas, Moritz Hardt, and Arvind Narayanan
  • The Emerging Science of Machine Learning Benchmarks by Moritz Hardt

Grading

Modern statistical learning requires mastery on multiple levels: theory, methodology, and applications. While the main focus of this course lies in applications, the grading in this course is designed to evaluate students on all three elements. Specifically, your grade depends on three major components

  • 30% In-Class Midterm Exam
  • 30% Homework (4, Equally Weighted)
  • 40% Applied Final Exam (Prediction Contest)

Points will be aggregated for a maximum class score of 1000. Final grades will be curved upwards in accordance with relevant program, departmental, and college policies. (Final grades will not be curved downwards.) Roughly, the following portion of final grades are expected:

  • A Range (A to A-): <= 40%
  • B Range (B+ to B-): <= 40%
  • C- and Lower: <20%

In-Class Midterm (Theory and Concepts)

One in-class test, worth 300 points, will be given approximately two-thirds of the way through the semester. Each tests will have a mixture of multiple choice, short answer, and long-answer questions covering material covered in lecture, instructor notes, and assigned readings.

Rules to avoid opportunities for cheating will be distributed in class before the midterm. Please review the rules and let your instructor know before the exam if you need an accommodation regarding the rules. There will be zero tolerance for failure to abide by those rules.

No outside materials will be allowed during the tests. If necessary, the instructor will provide a sheet of key definitions and formulas.

Homework (Methodology)

Four problem sets, each worth a total of 75 points, will be assigned throughout the semester. These will cover applications of the methods discussed in class to a variety of data sets. Each homework assignment will be submitted online through Gradescope.

Each homework assignment will be due at 5:00pm on Friday, with an automatic grace period lasting until the following Monday at 8:00am. During the grace period, course staff will not typically answer questions, so make sure to ask any questions well in advance of the deadlines. You do not need to request this grace period; it will be automatically applied.

Other than the standard grace period, no late work will be accepted on any homework outside of exceptional circumstances. (Non-hospitalizing illness, extracurricular conflicts, or commitments for other courses are not considered exceptional, so plan ahead.) If you have a foreseeable circumstance that you believe warrants an extension, please contact the instructor or the TA well in advance; no extensions will be given for foreseeable circumstances without specific prior written authorization.

After each assignment is submitted, the instructor or TAs may require students to meet and to discuss their submissions. Students who are not able to clearly demonstrate understanding of their submissions will be significantly penalized.

Applied Final Exam (Applications)

In lieu of a traditional final exam, STAT 3106 has a semester-long “prediction competition” in which students will develop, tune, and deploy machine learning algorithms on a difficult instructor-provided data set. Your grade in this competition will be based on three components:

  • 200 Points: Prediction Accuracy
  • 100 Points: In-Class Presentation
  • 100 Points: Final Report

Prediction Accuracy

Prediction accuracy will be assessed using a private Kaggle Competition. The instructor will provide Kaggle with a private test set on which your predictions will be evaluated. Kaggle will further divide the test set into a public leaderboard and a private leaderboard. You will be able to see your accuracy on the public leaderboard but final scores will be based on private leaderboard accuracy so don’t overfit.

You will be allowed to submit only two sets of predictions to Kaggle per day so use them wisely. At the end of the competition, you will be able to select two sets of predictions to be used for your final evaluation (taking the better of the two) so track your submissions carefully.

Further details about the data set, prediction metrics and grading scheme will be provided later in the course.

In-Class Presentation

During the final full week of class1, students will give a 6 minute presentation, primarily focused on the models and methods they used to build their predictive model. Details of presentations, including the presentation schedule, will be made available later in the course.

Students will present in an order established by the instructor and should expect to attend course on both presentation days.

If you have a foreseeable circumstance that requires you to present on a particular date, please contact the instructor or the TA well in advance. Failure to present on the assigned day will incur an automatic 0 on this portion of your grade.

Final Report

After the competition ends, you will submit a final report of no more than 8 pages2 (10-12 point, one-and-one-half- or double-spaced) providing an After-Action Report of your competition performance. This report should focus on three topics:

  • Are there any systematic errors in model predictions that need to be addressed before this model could be applied broadly. (E.g., is it systematically low or high in a particular subgroup; does it under-predict for especially high outcome samples; etc?)
  • What insights into the underlying data can be gleaned from the model? E.g., are certain features especially important for making predictions? Are certain features which you would expect to be important not actually particularly important?
  • What steps did you take that were particularly helpful to maximizing predictive performance? Or, what parts of your model development cycle were weak and could be the most improved?

Note that this report is not solely focused on predictive performance. Analysis that dives deep into the underlying structure of the data and generates novel insights will score as well (or perhaps even better) than a highly performant but noninterpretable model.

To assist in developing this After-Action Report, the instructor will provide non-anonymized versions of the data (as well as a mapping to the anonymized data) after the Kaggle competition ends.

The report should include all code used to prepare the data and train and predict from the best performing models in an Appendix. Significant penalties will be applied if the instructor is unable to reliably reproduce your predictions. (You may choose to submit this Appendix in the form of an iPython Notebook, Quarto document, Docker container, etc. to maximize reproducibility.) Note that this appendix does not count against your 8 page limit. You will submit your appendix either via an emailed Zip archive or a link to a public code hosting platform such as GitHub.

Disability Accommodations

Students should review the material at https://www.college.columbia.edu/rightsandresponsibilities. If you believe that you might have a disability that requires accommodation, you should contact Disability Services (Columbia) or CARDS (Barnard).

Academic Integrity

Students should review Columbia College’s Academic Integrity standards at https://www.cc-seas.columbia.edu/academic-integrity and consult with the instructor if they have any questions.

Problem Sets: Students are allowed (and encouraged!) to work with each other on problem sets. Additionally, students may use free tiers of generative AI software or any other free web resources to complete any aspect of the problem sets. Students will be required to list all resources used (including generative AI tools or classmates) at the end of each problem set.

Tests: No external resources may be used.

Applied Final Exam: Students are allowed to work with each other on the prediction competition (but keep in mind that this is a competition and your grade is based on your outcome relative to your classmates). Additionally, students may use free tiers of generative AI software or any other free web resources to develop and implement predictive models. Generative AI may not be used to write the final report. Students will be required to list all resources used(including generative AI tools or classmates) as part of their final submission.

Punishment for violations of relevant academic integrity standards range from a 50% penalty on the assignment to an automatic failure of the course. In matters of academic integrity, it is far better to ask permission than forgiveness.

Tentative Schedule

Class sessions will alternate between traditional “lecture” sessions, focused on key concepts and methods in machine learning, and “lab” sessions, focused on application and interpretation of methods to real data sets. This is not a hard distinction: some applications may be demonstrated in “lecture” sessions and ideas will be introduced and reviewd in “lab” sessions.

For each class session, suggested “pre-reading” will be assigned from one of the course textbooks, typically ISLR/ISLP. This is optional, but strongly recommended, for students who want to have prior exposure to the fundamentals of that day’s topic before lecture.
