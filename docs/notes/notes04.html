Outline:

Today we’re going to look a bit more closely at the reasons why an ML system can fail. Before we do so, let’s review our basic framework of ML:

\[\begin{align*} \hat{f} &= \argmin_{f \in \Fcal} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n L(f(\bX_i), Y_i) \tag{ERM} \\ f_* = \argmin_{f \in \Fcal} \E[L(f(\bX), Y)] \tag{Optimal $f_*$ Minimizes Test Error} \\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n L(f(\bX_i), Y_i) \approx \E[L(f(\bX), Y)] \text{LLN} \end{align*}\]

Reviewing our pieces:

  • In the supervised context, we measure success on a loss function \(L\), where smaller is better
  • The best possible prediction function (within the category \(\Fcal\)), \(f_*\), is the one that minimizes our test error, i.e., our error on new data
  • Because we can’t actually ever know our true test error, we instead typically pick our guess \(\hat{f}\) as the best predictor (within the category \(\Fcal\)) on our training data, i.e., the one with the smallest empirical (training) risk
  • Hopefully, the empirical risk (training error) is a good proxy for the population risk (test error) and \(f_* \approx \hat{f}\)

This story seems coherent, but there are lots of places it can break!

  • Is the loss function \(L\) a true measure of what we actually want to happen? (Think of reward hacking / alignment issues, though these are less common in the purely numerical context.)

  • Is \(R_n(f)\) actually a good proxy for \(R(f)\)? Any time we are estimating something from data, we have to worry about sampling problems:

    • Is our training data representative of the (future) population we actually care about or is it biased in some way?
    • Is our training sample large enough that the LLN actually ‘kicks in’? If \(R_n(f)\) is super noisy, minimizing it might not actually get us very close to the minimum of \(R(f)\).

  • In the ERM context, picking a large \(\Fcal\) gives us a lower minimizer. How big is big enough?

    \[\Fcal_1 \subseteq \Fcal_2 \implies \min_{f \in \Fcal_2} \hat{R}_n(f) \leq \min_{f \in \Fcal_1} \hat{R}_n(f)\]

    and

    \[\Fcal_1 \subseteq \Fcal_2 \implies \min_{f \in \Fcal_2} R(f) \leq \min_{f \in \Fcal_1} R(f)\]

    (Confirm for yourself why these two statements are true.)

  • Does risk minimization actually mean we’re doing what we want? Under what conditions does \(R(f_1) \approx R(f_2)\) imply \(f_1 \approx f_2\)?

  • How well can we actually find \(\hat{f}\)? If \(L\) is complicated, our data set is large, or \(\Fcal\) is large, solving the ERM problem might not be easy.

Our goal over the next few classes is to look at several of these problems in turn. Some of them are things we can get a handle on mathematically, while others require more qualitative / problem-specific analysis.

Bounding the Generalization Gap

Suppose we have the right (best) predictor \(f_*\) and our ERM-estimated predictor \(\hat{f}\). We know that

\[\hat{R}_n(\hat{f}) \leq \hat{R}_n(f_*)\]

and

\[R(\hat{f}) \geq R(f_*)\]

The first is true by ERM - \(\hat{f}\) minimizes the training error \(\hat{R}_n\) - while the second is true by definition - the best predictor \(f_*\) is defined as the minimizer of \(R(\cdot)\). While this seems like a nice tidy story, this actually isn’t quite enough to give us what we want:

\[ R(\hat{f}) \leq R(f_*) + \text{A Little Bit} \]

That is, we hope that in the future / on the test set, \(\hat{f}\) will be nearly optimal. To ensure something like this holds, we need to start unpacking the sources of error. Let’s start with the simplest:

  • \(\Fcal\) might not actually contain the true relationship. For example \(\Fcal\) might be the set of all lines (as in OLS), but the real relationship might be quadratic. In this case, even if we get the best possible line, the misfit between \(\Fcal\) and the real world is an unavoidable source of error. Let’s call \(f_{**}\) the true best possible predictor - from all of math, not just \(\Fcal\). We then have

    \[R(\hat{f}) - R(f_{**}) = \left(R(\hat{f}) - R(f_*)\right) + \underbrace{\left(R(f_*) - R(f_{**})\right)}_{\text{Systematic / Approximation Error}}\]

    We can’t say much about this systematic error yet, other than to note that a bigger \(\Fcal\) will hopefully get \(f_*\) closer to \(f_{**}\).

  • Turning to the first term, we have

    \[ R(\hat{f}) - R(f_*) = \underbrace{\left(R(\hat{f}) - \hat{R}_n(\hat{f}) \right)}_{V_1} + \underbrace{\left(\hat{R}_n(\hat{f}) - \hat{R}_n(f_*)\right)}_B + \underbrace{\left(\hat{R}_n(f_*) - R(f_*)\right)}_{V_2}\]

    We know that \(B \leq 0\) - why? So we can change this to an inequality like

    \[ R(\hat{f}) - R(f_*) \leq \underbrace{\left(R(\hat{f}) - \hat{R}_n(\hat{f}) \right)}_{V_1} + \underbrace{\left(\hat{R}_n(f_*) - R(f_*)\right)}_{V_2}\]

    But what can we do with these two \(V\) terms? Well, if we look closely at them both, they measure ‘how far can \(\hat{R}_n\) be from \(R\) for a given \(f\)’. It’s quite hard to say how much the empirical risk of a specific function can vary from its mean (Why is \(\E[\hat{R}_n] = R?\)) but we can generally get an upper bound on this quantity, so let’s just look at the maximum it can be:

    \[ \begin{align*} R(\hat{f}) - R(f_*) &\leq \underbrace{\left(R(\hat{f}) - \hat{R}_n(\hat{f}) \right)}_{V_1} + \underbrace{\left(\hat{R}_n(f_*) - R(f_*)\right)}_{V_2} \\ &\leq \left|R(\hat{f}) - \hat{R}_n(\hat{f}) \right| + \left|\hat{R}_n(f_*) - R(f_*)\right| \\ &\leq 2 \max_{f' \in \Fcal} \left|R(f') - \hat{R}_n(f') \right| \end{align*}\]

So if we put this all together, we get

\[ \begin{align*} R(\hat{f}) &\leq R(f_{**}) + \left|R(f_*) - R(f_{**})\right| + 2 \max_{f' \in \Fcal} \left|R(f') - \hat{R}_n(f') \right| \\ &= R(f_{**}) + \min_{f \in \Fcal} \left|R(f) - R(f_{**})\right| + 2 \max_{f' \in \Fcal} \left|R(f') - \hat{R}_n(f') \right| \\ \end{align*}\]

This is a key relationship, so let’s look very closely at it:

  • On the left hand side, we have the test error we want to control. All of our eventual choices should be made to try to make this as small as possible, but we can’t really know it exactly, so we will make do by making our upper bound (on the right hand side) as small as possible.

  • The first term on the right-hand side, \(R(f_{**})\) is the risk associated with the Platonic ideal of prediction; this irreducible error is just a fact of nature and so we just take it as is. Sometimes, we can’t do well on a task because it’s just hard. (E.g., predicting social behavior from genetics, there’s definitely a little signal there, but even the best possible genetic model can’t account for all the social factors that make us who we are.)

  • The second term on the right-hand side, \(R(f_*) - R(f_{**})\), is a systematic or approximation error term. Even if we got the very best element of \(\Fcal\), it can’t be a perfect fit. Here, we are the source of the problem. If we could use a ‘better’ (bigger) \(\Fcal\), our error would be smaller. But, as we will soon see, this isn’t a painless choice.

    Because this term measures ‘misfit’, it is also often called ‘bias’ in statistical lingo. (This isn’t usually bias in the sense of prejudice, but it can be in some problems; it’s closer to bias in the sense of being a little off center / slanted that you might hear in engineering or cooking.)

  • The third term on the right hand side, \(\max_{f' \in \Fcal} \left|R(f') - \hat{R}_n(f') \right|\), is a measure of sampling or estimation variability and it attempts to capture how far our estimate of performance will be from true performance. If this is small, then picking \(\hat{f}\) with good training performance should guarantee good out-of-sample performance. But if it is large, we don’t have any such guarantee.

    Note that sampling variability is not bad per se: we could (theoretically) hope to do better than we saw in the training data, but since we picked \(\hat{f}\) to be optimal on the training data, it seems pretty unlikely that we’ll beat it ‘by chance.’

How then can we control

\[ 2\max_{f' \in \Fcal} \left|R(f') - \hat{R}_n(f') \right|\]

? Obviously, in a perfect world, we would just have lots of samples fo \(\hat{R}_n \approx R\) for all \(f\) (LLN) and we’d be happy. In practice, we don’t just have a ‘give me more data’ option, so we have to look elsewhere. If \(\Fcal\) were smaller, this quantity would be smaller; and larger \(\Fcal\) will ceteris paribus give us a larger value. So minimizing this term makes us want to pick as small an \(\Fcal\) as possible.

So here we have our dilemma: we want \(\Fcal\) to be as large as possible to make the approximation error small, but at the same tiem we want \(\Fcal\) to be as small as possible to make the sampling error small. Sadly, we can’t have both… More data is the only ‘free win’, but that’s typically easier said than done.

Aside: Is \(R(f_{**})\) real?

You might ask whether \(R(f_{**})\) is actually real or whether it’s just a different type of approximation error. This is a bit philosophical (does true randomness exist or do we just need ever more complex models?) so we won’t get into it here. It’s not a term that will drive our decision making, so arguing whether it is exactly 0 or some other unknown, unknowable, and uncontrollable number doesn’t matter too much at the end of the day.

Exact Decomposition

The above decomposition works for any loss function, but it turns out the math is especially nice if we use an MSE loss.

Specifically, let’s look at several quantities here:

  • \(Y\): the true label we want to predict
  • \(\E[Y|X]\): the optimal predictor under MSE loss (\(f_{**}\) above)
  • \(f_*\): the best \(\mathcal{F}\)-class predictor
  • \(\hat{f}\): the element of \(\mathcal{F}\) we wind up using.

In this case, we get the exact relationship:

\[\begin{align*} \E[(Y - \hat{f}(X))^2|X] &= \E[(Y - \E[Y|X])^2|X] + \E[(\E[Y|X] - f_*(X))^2|X] + \E[( f_*(X) - \hat{f}(X))^2|X] \\ &= \sigma_Y^2 + \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} \end{align*}\]

So here our terms are perfectly additive in a “Pythagorean” kind of way. We see that we have three terms:

  • Irreducible Error: doesn’t depend on anything we have any control over
  • Approximation Error / Bias: depends only on our choice of \(\mathcal{F}\) (which determines \(f_*\))
  • Sampling Error / Variance: driven the the randomness in our data leading to different \(\hat{f}\) on different data sets.

Other loss functions will follow this relationship approximately and it’s good for your intuition, even if it’s only exactly true (as equality, rather than some inequalities) for MSE.

Tradeoff?

The above is a bias-variance decomposition. It is a fundamental law of how we do the accounting for these types of problems. We haven’t ‘created or destroyed’ error, we’ve simply put it into buckets that we hope to better understand.

Later in the course, we will see that sometimes (but not always), we can adjust the size of \(\Fcal\) so that \(\text{Bias}^2\) goes up by 4 while Variance goes down by 9. This is a clear win! But we don’t always have this option.

One of the most open and active questions in modern ML theory is why sometimes picking a bigger \(\Fcal\) seems to help on both bias and variance (or at least not hurt). Answers to this are many–we might not actually be measuring things correctly, this is ‘worst case’ theory that doesn’t apply to real life, there are some other factors at play that drive results here–and all true to one degree or another.

For us, in the simple scalar supervised context: it’s sufficient to say that we are conservative ‘worst case’-minded in theory, but to hope that theory will still provide some practical advice for real problems.

Finally, note that essnetially none of these quantities can be known in practice. Next time, we will look at practical approaches for actually measuring performance.
