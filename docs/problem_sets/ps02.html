Introduction

As you work through these, be sure to consider the relative pros and cons of each approach. As we’ve emphasized throughout the semester, there is no universally best method – “no free lunch” – for any given task and the best method for a task will be a complex function of data types, your goals, data size, and personal preferences.

1 ML Best Practices Question (16 Points)

Consider the scenario below in I describe a hypothetical application of ML. There are (at least) 5 issues in how the ML system is developed, tested, and deployed. Identify four distinct issues and, for each issue you identify, describe:

  • The mistake that was made (1 points)
  • The likely adverse impact arising from the mistake (1 points)
  • What should have been done instead (2 points)

(This is what lawyers might call an “Issue Spotter” question.)

Scenario: Hamilton, Butler, and Low (HBL) is an elite management consulting firm, which has long-prided itself on hiring only the most elite and high-achiving Ivy League undergraduates. To be more ‘AI forward’, to reduce historical hiring biases, to eliminate the thousands of (otherwise billable) partner hours spent vetting undergraduate applications, HBL tasked its People Analytics team with developing an automated screening engine dubbed Project ROAR (Rapid Objective Applicant Ranking).

Historically, HBL’s recruitment had relied heavily on informal alumni networking, on-campus partner dinners, and unstructured behavioral interviews, which leadership acknowledged had favored traditional feeder networks and a predominantly male candidate pool. To cast a wider, more meritocratic net, the project sponsor announced that ROAR would serve as an automated, interview-free gatekeeper for all incoming applicants: any candidate whose predicted first-year job performance score exceeded 78 on HBL’s standard continuous 1 to 100 rating scale would automatically receive an offer for a final-round interview slot, while those below 78 would be rejected without additional human review.

The analytics team began by querying internal HR records from 2017 to 2021, compiling complete digital application files and subsequent first-year review scores for 1,400 junior associates hired under the firm’s historical recruiting regime. To capture every possible objective signal, the data engineers extracted hundreds of resume keywords, collegiate club memberships, undergraduate major indicators, and assigned onboarding salary tiers, while imputing missing collegiate transcript grades with the global mean across the entire 5-year archive. Before fitting any models, the team normalized all continuous predictors using the mean and variance computed over the complete 1,400-employee database, and ran a univariate screening step across all columns to pre-filter down to the top 200 most correlated features. They adopted Mean Squared Error (MSE) as their primary accuracy measure, reasoning that quadratic penalties on score discrepancies were mathematically tractable, captured the ‘big miss is a big loss’ phenomenon in hiring, and cleanly mirrored standard regression theory.

For the predictive engine, the team evaluated high-dimensional linear models and forward stepwise polynomial regression. To tune the model, the lead analyst configured the forward stepwise algorithm to continually add polynomial terms and pairwise interactions until the in-sample Residual Sum of Squares (RSS) ceased to drop by more than 0.25%, celebrating when the model achieved an in-sample \(R^2\) of 0.91. When fitting Ridge and Lasso models as benchmarks, the engineers selected the penalty parameter \(\lambda\) that maximized in-sample explained variance on the training set, concluding that because \(\lambda \to 0\) delivered the lowest error, regularization was unhelpful and could be safely omitted. To evaluate the pipeline prior to deployment, the team partitioned the 1,400 past employees into an 80/20 train/validation split using uniform random sampling across the rows. Upon finding that the model achieved a validation RMSE of just 3.4 points on the 1–100 scale, the team declared ROAR production-ready.

The system was launched for the 2024–2025 recruiting cycle, where HBL actively marketed its new “resume-blind, network-free” process to hundreds of non-traditional campuses, successfully attracting an applicant pool that was majority female and demographically unprecedented for the firm.

Pure GD is rarely used for large-scale machine learning as:

Instead, stochastic (mini-batch) gradient descent and its various ‘smart step size’ variants are commonly used. 3.3 Early Stopping of First Order Methods

When fitting first-order methods, it is typically not necessary to run things ‘all the way to the end’. Getting the first several decimal places is usually enough: the final decimal places exist ‘within the noise’ and give functionally equivalent predictions. (Put another way, the steps required to get the 7th digit of precision are functionally pointless and don’t typically improve predictions by a measurable amount.) This motivates early-stopping: the idea that we can stop a first-order method when its predictions stop improving, rather than running until the weights fully stabilize.

To implement early-stopping in practice, it is important to have a hold-out (validation) set. After each (S)GD update, prediction error on the hold-out set is computed. When it stops decreasing (or, heaven forbid, starts increasing!), we stop the first-order method.

Adapt your GD and SGD methods to implement this type of early stopping. To do so:

  1. Split your data into ‘training’ and ‘validation’ components
  2. Modify your update loop to compute the training and validation errors at each step and to track them
  3. Plot the training and validation errors over the course of model fitting.5
  4. Annotate where you would perform ‘early stopping’ and compare the computational cost and prediction performance of the ‘early stopped’ model with the ‘fully fitted’ version.
