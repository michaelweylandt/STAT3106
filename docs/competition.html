STAT 3106 - Course Prediction Competition

In lieu of a final exam, STAT 3106 has a prediction competition worth 40% of your final grade. The 400 total points associated with this competition will be awarded as:

  • 200 Points: Prediction Accuracy
  • 100 Points: In-Class Presentation
  • 100 Points: Final report

Your task in this competition is to predict residential property valuations in a major US city (not NYC).

ImportantUse only Provided Information

You may not use any external data beyond what the instructor provides. You will be required to attest to this in your final submission. Students found to use data outside instructor-provided files will receive a zero on all grades related to the course competition and will likely fail the course as a result.

You may read published research about residential real estate to gain qualitative background information, but you may not access their data. You will be required to cite all background reading in your final report.

Background

In the United States, local taxes are typically collected using an ad valorem tax on real property (land and buildings). Ad valorem means that the tax amount is determined as a fixed percentage of the value of the property, almost always taken to be the fair market value.1 Hence, to determine taxes, government authorities must undertake an annual assessment process.

Typically, assessment procedes as follows:

  1. Early in the year, the local assessment authority estimates the fair market value of each property in the relevant area and informs the owner
  2. The owner then has the option to contest (variously called “grieve”, “protest”, or “challenge”) the assessment in front of a neutral party
  3. The neutral party then assigns an updated valuation to contested properties
  4. The local assessment authority collects unchallenged and new assessment values and uses them to create property tax bills
  5. The assessment authority sends tax bills to the relevant tax collector who then collects taxes from property owners

For large regions, this assessment process typically relies (at least in part) on some sort of automated process. In this task, you will be predicting the (finalized) assessment values of residential properties for 2024. You will be provided with various details about the underlying land and improvements (built structures).

While assessment values are typically relatively constant over time (growing at a rate proportional to local housing prices), large assessment shifts can occur if the property is remodeled, rebuilt, or otherwise significantly altered. In the region of interest, assessments are the sum of a building assessment and a land assessment, which may not move in lockstep.

Example Predictions

To submit your predictions, you need to upload them to Kaggle through the web interface. A link to register for the course competition will be distributed through Brightspace. Note that you must use your @columbia.edu email so that I can match your Kaggle ID with your student records. If this is a problem, please contact the instructor directly.

You will submit your predictions as a two-column CSV with columns:

  • KAGGLE_ID: Taken directly from the test set
  • Y: Your predictions

Kaggle will use the KAGGLE_ID to align your submissions with the test set, so your rows do not need to be in the same order as the test set file.

A (comically simple) model would simply use the average property value for for all predictions. In R,

When advertising homes, real estate agents and data platforms regularly advertise homes by ‘price per square foot.’ This is obviously a very simplified model (essentially univariate linear regression), but it does capture the very rough truth that, ceteris paribus, a larger home will sell for me. We can implement this in R as follows:

library(tidyverse)
library(broom)

TRAINING_DATA <- read_csv(
   "https://michael-weylandt.com/STAT3106/data/competition_fall_2026/students/fall_2026_training.csv.gz", 
   show_col_types = FALSE)

TEST_DATA <- read_csv(
   "https://michael-weylandt.com/STAT3106/data/competition_fall_2026/students/fall_2026_test_X.csv.gz", 
   show_col_types = FALSE)

OLS_SQFT_MODEL <- lm(Y ~ TOT_MAIN_SF, data=TRAINING_DATA)

augment(OLS_SQFT_MODEL, newdata=TEST_DATA) |> 
   select(.fitted, KAGGLE_ID) |> 
   rename(Y=.fitted) |> 
   write_csv("sqft_model_for_kaggle_r.csv")

Python can be implemented similarly:

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

training_data = pd.read_csv(
    "https://michael-weylandt.com/STAT3106/data/competition_fall_2026/students/fall_2026_training.csv.gz"
)

test_data = pd.read_csv(
    "https://michael-weylandt.com/STAT3106/data/competition_fall_2026/students/fall_2026_test_X.csv.gz"
)

# Note: scikit-learn expects features (X) to be a 2D array/DataFrame, 
# hence the double brackets even though we're only using a single column
X_train = training_data[["TOT_MAIN_SF"]]
y_train = training_data["Y"]

ols_sqft_model = LinearRegression().fit(X_train, y_train)

X_test = test_data[["TOT_MAIN_SF"]]
test_data["Y"] = ols_sqft_model.predict(X_test)

# Extract just the two columns Kaggle expects
submission = test_data[["Y", "KAGGLE_ID"]]
submission.to_csv("sqft_model_for_kaggle_py.csv", index=False)

Grading

Prediction Accuracy (200 Points)

Prediction accuracy will be assessed using Kaggle Competitions. I will provided Kaggle with a private test set on which your predictions will be evaluated. Kaggle will further divide the test set into a public leaderboard and a private leaderboard. You will be able to see your accuracy on the public leaderboard but final scores will be based on private leaderboard accuracy so don’t overfit.

You will be allowed to submit only two sets of predictions to Kaggle per day so use them wisely. At the end of the competition, you will be able to select two sets of predictions to be used for your final evaluation (taking the better of the two) so track your submissions carefully.

Kaggle will close for submissions at 11:59pm on Sunday December 06, 2026 for this class. Make sure your final submissions have been made in time.

Grading

Grades for this portion will be assigned based on your performance relative to both the instructor provided baselines and the best score obtained by your fellow students.3 Specifically, grades for this portion will be computed as:

\[\text{Grade} = 20\% + 80\%\left(\frac{\text{Your Best RMSE} - \text{Baseline Model RMSE} }{\text{Class Best RMSE} - \text{Baseline Model RMSE} }\right)\] Depending on the final distribution of scores, a more generous grading formula may be used.4

That is, your grade will essentially be a ‘rescaled’ \(R^2\) where the best performance in the class is 100% and everyone else is some fraction thereof. (Note that the specific formula used means the top performance automatically gets 100% on this component and that any submission beating the instructor’s baseline will get at least a 20.)

You will be allowed to submit only two sets of predictions to Kaggle per day so use them wisely. At the end of the competition, you will be able to select two sets of predictions to be used for your final evaluation (taking the better of the two) so track your submissions carefully.

Final Report (100 Points)

By Wednesday December 23, 2026 at 23:59 PM, each competition unit (either an individual or a team) will submit a final report of no more than 10 pages (10-12 point, one-and-a-half or double-spaced) providing an After-Action Report of their competition performance. This report should focus on three topics:

    1. Are there any systematic errors in model predictions that need to be addressed before this model could be applied broadly. (E.g., is it systmatically low or high in a particular subgroup; does it under-predict for especially high outcome samples; etc?)
    1. What insights into the underlying data can be gleaned from the model? E.g., are certain features especially important for making predictions? Or are certain features which you would expect to be important not actually particularly important?
    1. What steps did your team take that were particularly helpful to maximizing predictive performance? Or, what parts of your model development cycle were weak and could be the most improved?6

Note that this report is not solely focused on predictive performance. Analysis that dives deep into the underlying structure of the data and generates novel insights will score as well (or perhaps even better) than a highly performant but noninterpretable model.

To assist in developing this After-Action Report, the instructor will provide non-anonymized versions of the data (as well as a mapping to the anonymized data) after the Kaggle competition ends.

The report should include all code used to prepare the data and train and predict from the best performing models in an Appendix. Significant penalties may be applied if the course staff is unable to reliably reproduce your predictions. (You may choose to submit this Appendix in the form of an iPython Notebook, Quarto document, Docker container, etc to maximize reproducibility.) This appendix does not count against your 10 page limit.

You may, but are not required to, share your code with the instructor via an emailed Zip file or link to a public code hosting platform such as GitHub.
